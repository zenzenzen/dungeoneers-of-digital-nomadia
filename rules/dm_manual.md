# Dungeoneers GM Manual

> The world is on fire, the coworking password changed again, and somehow this is still game night.

This is the short-form GM guide for running Dungeoneers of Digital Nomadia as a 1-2 hour flash session between friends. It uses the same rules language as the core system, so a one-shot can grow into a campaign without everybody having to relearn the math they were barely pretending to know in the first place.

For exact combat math, condition wording, and longer faction play, use:

- `rules/combat_system.md`
- `rules/influence_system.md`
- `rules/status_afflictions.md`

## What This Game Feels Like

Dungeoneers plays like a social-fantasy pressure cooker. It is fast, petty, dramatic, weirdly tactical, and painfully recognizable. The characters are competent enough to make a mess on purpose and fragile enough to be ruined by one bad group chat screenshot.

The tone should feel:

- sharp, not cruel
- cynical, but still affectionate about human stupidity
- heightened and absurd, but emotionally legible
- more "I cannot believe this is happening again" than "everyone here is trash"

Run scenes like a comedy of status, logistics, ego, hustle, and consequences. The joke lands harder when the stakes are mechanically real.

## 10-Minute Setup

If you are starting cold, do this and begin.

1. Pitch the premise in one sentence.
   Examples: "Tonight you are trying to survive a doomed startup mixer in Taipei." "Tonight you need one permit, two allies, and zero public humiliations before dawn."
2. Pick a location and one active problem.
   Good flash-session locations are a coworking space, visa office, rooftop party, hostel bar, retreat center, airport gate, night market, or livestream.
3. Give every player a class, background, and one personal agenda.
   Personal agendas should be short and selfish: get invited in, get paid, get revenge, get closure, get out clean.
4. Name the three most important NPCs or factions.
   One helps, one blocks, one makes everything worse by being themselves.
5. Set one session clock.
   Use 4 segments for a 1-hour session or 6 segments for a 2-hour session.

If you need a faster start, skip deep character shopping and let each player choose:

- one class
- one background
- one item they absolutely should not be trusted with

That is enough to ruin an evening productively.

## 3-Scene Flash Session Structure

The default flash session is three scenes. Keep it moving. Do not build a ten-room dungeon when what you actually have is one evening, four friends, and a shared desire to watch a fake founder implode.

### Scene 1: The Hook

Time: 15-25 minutes

Start in motion. Somebody already needs something. Somebody important is already annoyed. Somebody else has mistaken confidence for authority.

Use Scene 1 to establish:

- the immediate goal
- the room's social physics
- the first obvious obstacle
- one thing the party can win quickly if they commit

End Scene 1 with a complication, not a conclusion.

Examples:

- The permit is available, but only if the party gets past the Embassy Official before lunch.
- The investor is interested, but Crypto Bro Kevin has already started poisoning the room.
- The party finds the target, but they are protected by `Misinformation`, social rank, and a truly demonic seating chart.

### Scene 2: The Escalation

Time: 20-35 minutes

This is the scene where people start spending resources and revealing how unwell they are. Put pressure on time, status, access, or public perception. Split loyalties. Force movement between zones. Let players choose whether they want a clean win, a messy win, or a principled failure.

Good Scene 2 complications:

- the wrong person overhears
- an ally wants payment now
- the room changes from `Table` scale to `Scene` scale
- a private problem becomes public
- a faction takes interest
- the players succeed, but gain `Heat`

### Scene 3: The Finale

Time: 20-30 minutes

The finale should answer one question: who leaves changed, exposed, defeated, promoted, ghosted, cancelled, sponsored, or weirdly vindicated?

Make the final scene about one of these:

- a public showdown
- a race against a clock
- a moral compromise
- a reputation kill-shot
- a negotiation where everyone is technically smiling

The finale ends when the objective is secured, ruined, abandoned, or made somebody else's problem.

## Abbreviated Rules for Flash Sessions

Use the core rules, but keep the visible layer light.

### Round Basics

Each round still gives:

- 1 Standard Action
- 1 Move Action
- 1 Swift Action
- 1 Immediate Action between turns

For a flash session, explain them like this:

- Standard: do the important thing
- Move: change position or context
- Swift: set up, boost, or react lightly
- Immediate: interrupt somebody's nonsense

If a player wants to do something stylish and plausible, let them. Translate it into the existing action types instead of saying no because the spreadsheet was not emotionally ready.

### Core Rolls

Use only three questions at the table:

- Are you attacking someone's composure or public standing? Roll against `Cringe Resistance`.
- Are you resisting a spell, trap, condition, panic effect, or humiliation wave? Roll the relevant save.
- Are two people directly pushing against each other? Run a contest.

Use `Vibe Check` for initiative. Use `Chill` as health. Hitting `0 Chill` means `Triggered`, which usually means the target loses the scene and cannot keep acting until recovery.

### Keep the Condition Set Small

For a 1-2 hour game, you usually only need these conditions on the table:

- `Triggered`
- `Embarrassed`
- `Distracted`
- `Doubt`
- `Blocked`
- `Silenced`
- `Surprised`
- `Receipts Collected`
- `Misinformation`

Only reach for the larger condition list when the joke and the tactic both improve.

### Quick Influence Handling

If a scene is about trust, access, favors, or public pressure, use the influence system in short form:

- `Trust`: do they believe you
- `Access`: can you get in front of them
- `Leverage`: what do you have on them
- `Heat`: how dangerous is the attention around this

For a flash session, do not track every relationship numerically unless the scene deserves it. Instead:

- pick the one track that matters most
- set a simple threshold of 2 or 3 successful pushes
- let failure increase `Heat`, close `Access`, or poison `Trust`

This keeps one-shot play quick while staying compatible with the full campaign rules.

### Quick Spell Rulings

Most spells already tell you what they are trying to do, even when the exact edge case reads like it was written during low blood sugar and a deadline spiral.

For flash sessions, resolve unclear spell text with these defaults:

- if it deals pressure directly, roll it like an attack against `Cringe Resistance`
- if it imposes a state, apply one named condition from `rules/status_afflictions.md`
- if duration is unclear, use 3 rounds in combat time or 1 scene beat in pure social time
- if the effect would trivialize the whole session, let it solve the local problem and create a new consequence, witness, or `Heat`

Good quick spell outcomes:

- a glamour gets the party past the line, but now the bouncer remembers their faces
- a debugging ritual removes `Misinformation`, but exposes who planted it
- a confidence buff grants temporary `Chill`, then leaves the target `Doubt`-shaken when it wears off

The rule of thumb is simple: let spells feel strong, strange, and socially expensive.

## Quick NPC Building

An NPC for flash play only needs five things:

- what they want right now
- what they are protecting
- one relevant stat edge
- one annoying move
- one way players can actually reach them

Use this template:

`Name`
`Role and vibe`
`Primary defense`: high `Cringe Resistance`, strong save, or influence insulation
`Signature move`: one attack, condition, or pressure tactic
`Pressure point`: vanity, debt, loneliness, ambition, guilt, greed, boredom, logistics

Examples:

**Embassy Official**
Wants order, protects procedure, high `Access` gatekeeping.
Signature move: `Delay You Politely`
Pressure point: public competence and paperwork correctness

**Crypto Bro Kevin**
Wants attention, protects status, weak to `Fact Check`.
Signature move: spread `Misinformation` and generate `Doubt`
Pressure point: being exposed in front of people he wants to impress

**Barista Kyle**
Wants peace, protects the line, medium `Trust`, low patience.
Signature move: passive-aggressive reprioritization
Pressure point: genuine respect and somebody finally ordering like a functional adult

## Quick Encounter Building

A flash-session encounter should have one objective, one main threat, and one complication. If you need a fight-like scene, build it from roles instead of stat blocks first.

Useful encounter roles:

- **Face**: high `Cringe Resistance`, controls the room
- **Controller**: hands out `Distracted`, `Doubt`, `Silenced`, or forced movement
- **Bruiser**: hits `Chill` hard and fast
- **Leech**: drains momentum, time, or Social Capital
- **Swarm**: weak individually, brutal in aggregate because the room joins in

Good flash-session encounter objectives:

- get the key NPC alone
- cross the room before the narrative turns
- survive three rounds of public scrutiny
- remove `Misinformation` before the finale
- escape with your dignity and at least one ally

If the players are stuck, change the objective before changing the math.

## Card-Ready GM Blocks

If you want material that can survive the jump from rulebook to app screen to printed card, keep your prep blocks short and structured.

### NPC Block

Use:

- `Name`
- `Role`
- `Primary Faction`
- `Wants`
- `Protects`
- `Defense`
- `Signature Move`
- `Pressure Point`
- `Reward / Fallout`

Example:

`Embassy Official | Obstacle | Bureaucracy Machine | Wants order | Protects procedure | Defense: high Access control | Signature Move: Delay You Politely | Pressure Point: public competence | Reward/Fallout: permit, favor, or Heat spike`

### Encounter Block

Use:

- `Name`
- `Scene Type`
- `Objective`
- `Main Threat`
- `Complication`
- `Win Condition`
- `Loss Condition`
- `Reward`

Example:

`The Networking Event | Social Combat | Collect 10 business cards and leave with dignity | Main Threat: Mansplainer | Complication: investor attention clock | Win: secure Access | Loss: gain Heat and Doubt | Reward: intro, job lead, or leverage`

### Location Block

Use:

- `Name`
- `Type`
- `Scene Tags`
- `Hazards`
- `Mechanical Hook`
- `What Changes Here`

Example:

`The Coworking Space | Office | judgment;remote_work;wifi | Hazards: panic_wifi;noise_judgment | Hook: loud failures become public immediately | Changes Here: Access is easy, privacy is fake`

These blocks are small enough for cards, searchable enough for a database, and flavorful enough that the setting still sounds like itself instead of a compliance document with neon lighting.

## Pacing Clocks

Clocks keep a short session from sagging into freeform chaos. Use visible clocks. Let people panic responsibly.

For flash sessions, use at most two:

- **Session Clock**: how close the night is to ending badly
- **Complication Clock**: how close a threat is to going loud, public, or irreversible

Recommended sizes:

- 4 segments for tight, messy, one-hour games
- 6 segments for roomier, two-hour games

Tick a clock when:

- players fail loudly
- players succeed with collateral damage
- time passes without decisive action
- an NPC escapes, posts, calls backup, or closes off access

Sample clocks:

- Security notices you
- The group chat turns on you
- The investor loses interest
- Immigration closes for the day
- The room realizes you are bluffing

## Consequences and Fallout

Every scene should change something permanent enough that the table cares.

After each scene, assign one or two consequences:

- lose `Chill`
- gain `Heat`
- shift `Trust`
- lose or gain `Access`
- create or spend `Leverage`
- apply a condition that survives the scene
- advance a faction clock
- break an alliance
- make the next scene public

Do not punish every success. Do make success expensive often enough that choices matter.

Good fallout is specific. "Things get worse" is weak. "The permit is approved but now the Bureaucracy Machine knows your face" is usable.

If the party wins decisively, reward them with one clean benefit:

- recovered `Chill`
- bonus `Access`
- reduced `Heat`
- a favor
- a quiet exit
- one blessed hour of nobody being weird at them

## Running the Tone

The setting is satirical, but the table should still feel safe, lively, and collaborative.

Aim jokes upward and outward:

- systems
- status games
- fake authenticity
- hustle mythology
- cowardice disguised as strategy
- people lying to themselves in increasingly expensive neighborhoods

Do not linger on cruelty. The characters can be messy, vain, delusional, and catastrophic without the game becoming mean. Let NPCs be ridiculous and recognizable, but give them motives beyond "be awful." Even the worst person in the room usually thinks they are being reasonable. That is where the comedy lives.

## Expanding to Campaign Play

The flash-session rules already scale. To continue into campaign play, do not replace the system. Add persistence.

Carry forward:

- current `Chill`, conditions, and unresolved grudges
- faction relationships
- `Trust`, `Access`, `Leverage`, and `Heat`
- debts, receipts, and favors
- campaign clocks
- location consequences

To expand cleanly:

1. Turn one-off NPCs into recurring faction anchors.
2. Track influence numerically instead of loosely when the relationship matters.
3. Let victories create new enemies, not just rewards.
4. Keep scene-based zones and action economy exactly the same.
5. Add downtime, recovery, travel, and longer-term reputation fallout between sessions.

Flash play is the pilot episode. Campaign play is what happens when nobody learned a lesson.

## GM Checklist

Before play:

- choose location
- choose active problem
- choose 3 important NPCs or factions
- set 1 or 2 clocks
- define what counts as winning tonight

During play:

- keep scenes short
- push consequences forward
- use `Cringe Resistance`, saves, and contests consistently
- tick clocks when the room changes
- end scenes on reversals

After play:

- note who gained `Trust`, `Access`, `Leverage`, or `Heat`
- write down unresolved conditions and grudges
- decide what the room remembers

If you do only that, you can run a one-shot tonight and still have a campaign on your hands by accident next week.
